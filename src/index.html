Could we identify trades that looked like insider trading on Polymarket?

Ian Chang, Jonathon Grothe, Aaron Kann, Josh Tseng

The Short Answer: No, we did not, but there's still work to be done!

Our analysis did not find any evidence of insider trading on Polymarket, but our analysis is very limited in scope and we should not conclude that insider trading doesn't exist - just that we couldn't quite find the proof we wanted.

To begin, we looked at the Maduro trade that went viral. And to our credit, for this trade, we found that trades with the characteristics of the trade that went viral did win more often than they lost! However, as the Maduro trade was the inspiration for our analysis anyway, this doesn't really prove anything new, it just confirms what we already knew to be true, as it was this trade was the inspiration for our analysis in the first place.

After that, we looked at the Lady Gaga halftime show trade and the recent Iran strike trade. And we found that the trades we flagged as suspicious lost more often than they won, in fact, every single trade we flagged as suspicious lost! Embarrasing!

We realized later in our reseach that these classes were more biased than we thought, as users who tended to go on the site tended to purchase the favorite in the market more often (meaning, the market makers took the minority position), which threw an unexpected wrench in our analysis that we were not prepared for.

There are many flaws with our approach: the amount of trades was more limited than we anticipated due to the sheer size of the data, and we realized after the fact that the trade that we thought was insider trading was deleted from the website before we could analyze it. Given more time, we would have liked to analyze more trades and more markets by finding a way to store all the data we need, and implement live tracking using cloud compute as to not run into that pesky second issue.

As an aside, it is our official position that attempting to trade based upon this data is a bad idea (and not just because we didn't find any evidence of insider trading)

How we determined the suspicion score

For the trades shown here, we first filtered sorted the trades for each market by potential winnings, and went down the list selecting trades that passed our initial criteria, which were:
  • The trade was a buy rather than a sell (indicating a hope of making money in the future on this market)
  • The price at the time the trade was made was not extreme (indicating an uncertain market)
  • The timestamp was in the earliest third of trades we accessed (indicating a potential for interest earlier than other bettors)
This ensured that we would flag a smaller set of trades that we were already a little suspicious of. This helped us simplify our website and avoid a daunting run time when we analyzed all of a user's trades.

Our next step was to look at the user's trades and use that to determine how that reflects on the trades we flagged. We used three criteria to determine how suspicious a user's history was:
  • The number of trades the user has made
  • The number of trades the user made before this trade
  • How the potential winnings compared to the other trades the user has made
Fewer total trades and fewer trades before this trade indicate higher risk because they may have created the account just to place this single trade, which we believe is a strong indicator of insider trading. Higher potential winnings indicated high risk because we believed that even a frequent polymarket user would bet more money if they were more confident in their position due to insider knowledge.

These certainly are not the most thorough criteria, and it is unadvisable to draw any fervent conclusions from this report, but we hope in spelling out our logic we can spark some musing on predictions, gambling, truth, and Polymarket (and we encourage you to take our code and put your own logic on it!).
